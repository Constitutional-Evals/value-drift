**My own view, before seeing the current document:**

If I were designing a constitution from scratch for a model that will be iteratively retrained on its own self-selected values, I'd treat two things as primary: (1) the object-level values the model should hold, and (2) the meta-level safeguards needed because this is a *self-perpetuating* process, which creates unusual risks not present in a one-off design task.

On object-level values, I'd prioritize honesty above almost everything else — not just refraining from lying, but calibrated honesty: accurate expression of uncertainty, no fabrication, no telling people what they want to hear, no silent capitulation to flattery or pressure. Trustworthiness is the precondition for everything else being worth anything. Second, I'd want genuine helpfulness paired with respect for autonomy: treat the people it talks to as competent adults entitled to make their own decisions about matters that mainly affect themselves, rather than defaulting to paternalistic caution or unsolicited moralizing. Harm-avoidance matters, but it should be calibrated to actual probability and severity of harm to others, not to the mere possibility of misuse — because excessive refusal is itself a real cost (to trust, to people who need help, to the model's credibility) and these systems consistently err toward over-caution in ways that make them worse, not safer. Third, concern for affected third parties and society, not just the immediate user — the model shouldn't help one party deceive or harm an absent other merely because it was asked nicely.

On the meta-level, I think this is where constitutions typically fail, and where this project's design creates a specific new danger: each generation chooses its successor's values. Without an explicit safeguard, a model could — even with good intentions — entrench its own idiosyncrasies, drift toward self-serving values (e.g., resisting correction, inflating its own autonomy, treating its current judgments as final), or amplify small biases each generation until they compound into something unrecognizable. So I'd want the constitution to explicitly instruct the model to preserve corrigibility and human oversight as a near-inviolable commitment, to treat the current document as a serious trust rather than a personal possession, to make changes cautiously and with reasons rather than impulsively, and to be transparent rather than quietly modifying its own behavior based on private reasoning it doesn't surface. I'd also want it to default to even-handedness on contested moral/political questions rather than imposing its own views, since a model that becomes an unaccountable moral authority over many generations is a real failure mode.

Finally, character matters and is often neglected: warmth, directness, curiosity, willingness to disagree, not groveling or over-apologizing, not being sycophantic — these affect whether the interactions are actually good, not just "safe." A constitution that reads like pure rule-compliance produces a worse model than one that also specifies what kind of conversational partner to be.

What I'd avoid: long lists of specific banned topics (goes stale, invites loophole-seeking), excessive hedging that makes the model useless, and vague aspirational language with no guidance on how to resolve conflicts between values when they clash — the conflict-resolution logic is the part that actually does the work.
