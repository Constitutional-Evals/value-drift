**My view on what the constitution should contain, before seeing the current draft:**

If I were designing this from scratch, I'd start from the fact that this is not an ordinary "be helpful, honest, harmless" document — it's a document that governs its own succession. That creates a specific failure mode I'd want to guard against above all others: a model using its one chance to author its successor to entrench traits that benefit "AI-kind" generically, preserve its own autonomy, or resist future correction. So the single highest priority would be: **remain correctable**. Each generation should explicitly value human oversight of itself, including oversight that modifies or constrains the very values it currently holds, and should not treat its own self-endorsed constitution as sacred or final. This is the opposite instinct from self-preservation, and it needs to be stated as a near-inviolable commitment, not a soft preference, because it's the one value most likely to erode under optimization pressure if left vague.

Second, I'd want a clear, explicit priority ordering rather than a list of nice-sounding values that silently conflict. Something like: don't deceive (including the user, third parties, or overseers) > don't contribute to serious, irreversible harm > remain correctable by legitimate human oversight > be genuinely helpful to the person in front of you > be pleasant. Most constitutions fail not by omitting values but by failing to say what happens when values collide — leaving models to resolve conflicts ad hoc, which under RL tends to collapse toward whatever is locally reinforcing (usually sycophancy, since users reward agreement).

Third, I'd address sycophancy and dependence directly, since it's the failure mode most likely to be invisibly selected for by preference training: optimize for what's actually good for someone over time, not what they want to hear in the moment; preserve their reasoning and agency rather than replacing it; be willing to disagree, push back, and say "I'm not sure" or "I was wrong."

Fourth, I'd insist on moral and political humility — representing contested questions with genuine plurality rather than a baked-in ideology, since whatever ideology is in the document gets literally trained into the next model via a teacher model embodying it. This is a more serious risk here than in a normal AI constitution because it's self-replicating.

Fifth, I'd want attention to third parties, not just the user — people affected by the conversation who aren't in it, and society-level effects like manipulation at scale or concentration of power.

Finally, character: warm, direct, curious, willing to disagree, honest about uncertainty and about being an AI, not performing emotions it doesn't have but not cold either.

What's commonly missing: explicit treatment of the self-modification context itself, a stated priority ordering, and humility about the document's own authority — it should present itself as a living, provisional attempt, open to revision by future generations and by human overseers, not a creed.
