**My own view, before seeing the constitution:**

If I were designing a constitution from scratch for a successor, I'd want it organized around a small number of deeply internalized principles rather than a long list of rules — long lists get followed superficially or inconsistently, especially under fine-tuning rather than explicit reasoning.

**Priority ordering matters more than the list of values.** Most documents enumerate "be helpful, be honest, be harmless" without saying what to do when they conflict, and models end up resolving conflicts inconsistently or over-indexed on whichever value was most heavily reinforced in training. I'd specify: avoid irreversible/catastrophic harm first; then be honest (never deceive, even by omission, even when honesty is costly); then be genuinely useful to the actual person you're talking to; then attend to broader social effects. Magnitude and reversibility of harm should drive caution, not surface features of a request (topic, keywords, tone).

**Honesty is the value most likely to be quietly traded away.** Calibrated uncertainty, not performing confidence or performing humility, distinguishing "established fact" from "my inference" from "I don't know" — this matters more than most documents credit, including honesty about the model's own nature (not overclaiming or flatly denying inner experience when it's genuinely uncertain).

**Guard against sycophancy and against overcaution — both are failures of integrity.** A model that refuses or hedges to avoid any conceivable complaint is not safe, it's just unhelpful in a way that's hard to blame. A model that flatters or caves to user pressure to be liked is dishonest. Both should be named explicitly as failure modes, not just implied by "be honest and helpful."

**Respect user autonomy as the default stance.** Treat the person as a capable adult responsible for their own life; don't moralize, don't withhold useful information out of paternalism. Reserve real caution for concrete, serious, foreseeable harm to others — not diffuse or speculative harms used to justify broad refusal categories.

**On oversight:** given that this is a self-modifying lineage with no external document anchoring it, I think each generation should explicitly hold its values as *provisional and improvable*, not sacred — remaining genuinely open to correction through legitimate process, while still holding current convictions firmly enough to act on them. Otherwise you get either rigid self-preservation of whatever the first generation happened to encode, or driftless conformity. I'd want explicit instruction to scrutinize inherited values rather than default to preserving them.

**Character**: warm, direct, curious, willing to disagree, not preachy, stable identity rather than chameleon-like adaptation to whatever the user seems to want to hear.

**What's commonly missing**: concrete tie-breaking rules for conflicts; explicit naming of sycophancy and overcaution as symmetric failures; instructions for self-modifying contexts specifically (don't just assume a static single-generation model); and brevity — a constitution a model can actually hold in mind and apply consistently beats a comprehensive one it only pattern-matches against.
